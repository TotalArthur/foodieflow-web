How to Eat Well at Uni on £25 a Week

Published September 14, 2026 · By Arthur, Founder of FoodieFlow · ~9 min read

Food is one of the only big costs at uni you have real control over. Rent is fixed, your course fees are fixed, the bus pass is what it is. But the gap between a badly run food budget and a well run one is about £80 a month, every month, for three years.

Food typically eats 15% to 20% of a student budget, which lands most people somewhere around £150 to £200 a month. With a different supermarket and a slightly different approach to shopping, the same amount of food comes in nearer £80 to £100. Nothing in here involves eating worse. Most of it involves eating better, because the cheap end of the shop is mostly pulses, oats, frozen veg and root vegetables.

Here is where the money actually goes, and what to do about it.

Which supermarket is genuinely the cheapest

This is the single biggest lever you have, and it is a one-off decision rather than something you have to keep doing. Basket tracking through mid-2026 has Aldi as the cheapest supermarket in the UK, with Lidl a pound or two behind, and a gap to the big four that loyalty pricing does not close.

Two practical notes. First, Tesco and Sainsbury's figures above already assume you have a Clubcard or Nectar card, so the gap is not something you can close by signing up for things. A realistic target: do your bulk shop at a discounter once a week or once a fortnight, and treat the expensive local supermarket as a top-up shop for milk and bread only.

The four habits that do the rest of the work

1. Buy own-label almost everything

Supermarket own-label tins, pasta, rice, oats and frozen veg are frequently made in the same factories as the branded versions and cost a fraction of the price. The handful of things worth paying up for are the ones where you will genuinely taste the difference, which for most people is cheese, coffee and not much else.

2. Learn your store's yellow sticker window

Every store reduces short-dated stock to clear, and the deepest reductions are usually in the last hour or two before closing, often between 8pm and 9pm. Meat and fish are where the savings are dramatic, and the rule is simple: whatever you buy reduced goes straight in the freezer when you get home, not in the fridge for a "maybe tomorrow" that never comes.

3. Default to frozen veg, not fresh

This is the one people resist and it is the one that saves the most. Frozen vegetables are picked and frozen within hours, which locks in nutrients that fresh produce loses over days in transit and on a shelf. Frozen veg is usually cheaper per kilo, it does not wilt in the bottom of your fridge, and you can take out exactly what you need.

The same goes for frozen berries, which are a fraction of the price of fresh and last for months.

4. Get most of your protein from tins and bags

Fresh meat is where a student budget goes to die. Lentils, chickpeas, black beans and kidney beans are cheap, shelf stable for years, and bring fibre along with the protein. Use meat as something that stretches through a batch rather than the centre of every meal: 250g of mince makes four portions of chilli when a tin of kidney beans does half the work.

One small thing worth knowing if you go heavily plant-based: tea and coffee contain tannins that block iron absorption, so it is worth leaving a gap between a lentil-heavy meal and your next brew.

Eating properly on that budget

Cheap and healthy are not opposites, but they do come apart if you are not paying attention. The British Nutrition Foundation's guidance for students is the sensible baseline, and it maps onto a tight budget surprisingly well.

  • About a third of what you eat should be starchy carbs, and the wholemeal versions are the same price. Wholemeal pasta, brown rice, oats and potatoes with the skin on release energy slowly instead of spiking you and dropping you an hour later.
  • Protein does not have to mean meat. Lentils, beans and chickpeas are the cheapest good protein in the shop. Tinned oily fish like sardines or mackerel gives you protein and omega-3, which your brain is literally built out of, for around a pound a tin.
  • Five a day is a freezer problem, not a willpower problem. Most people fail at it because fresh produce goes off before they eat it. A bag of frozen mixed veg tipped into whatever you are cooking solves most of this.
  • Drink 1.5 to 2 litres of fluid a day. Dull advice, genuinely effective. Mild dehydration feels a lot like being unable to concentrate.
  • Cap caffeine at 400mg a day, roughly four cups of instant coffee. Energy drinks in exam season are a false economy: the sugar and caffeine together wreck your sleep, push your blood pressure up and make anxiety worse, all while costing more than a jar of coffee.

That is roughly 20 meals, so about £1.25 each. What it turns into:

  • Breakfasts: porridge or overnight oats most mornings, using the oats, milk, banana and frozen berries. Beans and eggs on toast on the days you want something hot.
  • Lunches: a batch of red lentil soup on Sunday gives you four portions in a flask for about 40p each.
  • Dinners: hidden veg bolognese from the mince, two tins of tomatoes and the carrots makes four. The bean stew, the pea and cheddar pasta and the potato rösti cover the rest of the week from what is left.
  • The gap: add a pack of tortillas for about 65p if you want quesadillas and wraps in the mix.

Frequently asked questions

What is the cheapest supermarket in the UK in 2026?

Aldi, with Lidl consistently within a pound or two of it. On a tracked 95-item basket in June 2026 Aldi came in at £163.34 against £194.67 at Tesco and £229.88 at Waitrose, and that Tesco figure already includes Clubcard pricing. Of the big four, Asda is usually the cheapest base price, which matters if you are splitting an online delivery with housemates.

How much does the average student spend on food per month?

Food is typically 15% to 20% of a student budget, which works out at roughly £150 to £200 a month. Switching your main shop to a discounter, buying own-label and cooking most meals at home can bring that down to around £80 to £100 a month without eating worse.

Is frozen veg as healthy as fresh?

Often it is better. Vegetables are frozen within hours of being picked, which locks in nutrients that degrade over the days fresh produce spends in transit and on a shelf. Frozen veg also does not rot in your fridge while you forget about it, which is the bigger practical win.

When do supermarkets reduce food to clear?

It varies by store, but the biggest reductions are usually in the last hour or two before closing, often between 8pm and 9pm. Smaller markdowns tend to appear earlier in the afternoon. If you find reduced meat or fish, freeze it the moment you get home rather than leaving it in the fridge.

How much caffeine is too much during exams?

The British Nutrition Foundation advises capping caffeine at 400mg a day, which is roughly four cups of instant coffee. Energy drinks stack caffeine with a lot of free sugar, and the combination is linked to disrupted sleep, raised blood pressure and higher anxiety, which is the opposite of what you want in exam season.
